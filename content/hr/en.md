# HR interview guide

The HR interview checks motivation, behavior and fit, not code. Each question below has what the recruiter is really checking, how to build your answer, an example for a developer, and what to avoid. Replace the examples with your own stories: real details are what make an answer convincing.

## Before the interview

**Key ideas**

- **Research the company:** product, customers, recent news, tech stack, values. Prepare one sentence on why *this* company.
- **Reread the job offer** and match each main requirement with one example from your experience.
- **Prepare 5–6 stories** you can reuse: a challenge, a failure, a conflict, a success you are proud of, a time you learned fast, a time you helped the team.
- **The STAR method** for behavioral questions: **S**ituation (context, 1–2 sentences), **T**ask (your responsibility), **A**ction (what *you* did — the longest part), **R**esult (outcome, numbers if possible, what you learned).
- Answers of **1–2 minutes**. Speak about "I" for your actions, "we" for the team.
- Practice out loud. Record yourself once: you will hear filler words and answers that are too long.

**Traps**

- Memorizing answers word for word — you will sound like you are reciting. Remember the structure and key points.
- Criticizing a former employer, manager or colleague.
- Saying "I don't have an example" — use school projects, internships, freelance or volunteer work.

## About you

### "Tell me about yourself."

**What they want to know:** Can you present yourself clearly and briefly? Is your profile relevant to the job?

**How to answer** — present → past → future, about 1–2 minutes:

- **Present:** your current role or situation and your main skills.
- **Past:** 1–2 experiences or achievements that prove those skills.
- **Future:** why this job is the logical next step.

**Example answer:**

> I'm a full-stack developer with three years of experience, mostly with Angular and Spring Boot. At my current company I work on a B2B invoicing platform used by about 2,000 businesses; last year I led the migration of our front end to standalone components and cut the bundle size by 30%. Before that, I did a six-month internship where I discovered how much I like working close to users. Today I want to join a product company where I can own features end to end, and that's exactly what this position offers.

**Avoid:** your whole life story, personal details (age, family) unless relevant, reading your CV line by line.

### "Walk me through your CV."

**What they want to know:** The logic of your career path and the reasons behind each change.

**How to answer:**

- Go in chronological order, one or two sentences per step.
- For each step: what you did, one result, and why you moved on.
- Explain gaps briefly and positively (training, personal project, travel, family) — no need to over-justify.

**Example answer:**

> I studied software engineering, with a final-year project on a real-time delivery tracking app in Node.js. I then joined Company X as a junior developer, where I learned to work on a large codebase with code reviews and CI. After two years I wanted more responsibility on architecture, so I moved to Company Y, where I now design new services. Each step has brought me closer to a role like this one, where I can combine development and technical decisions.

**Avoid:** long explanations of every technology; sounding like you left jobs only because of problems.

### "Why did you choose this field?"

**What they want to know:** Genuine motivation — people who like their job learn faster and stay longer.

**How to answer:** a short, true story about what made you start, then what still motivates you today.

**Example answer:**

> I started programming in high school by building a small website for my family's shop, and I loved seeing people actually use something I made. That's still what motivates me today: solving a concrete problem and seeing the impact on users.

**Avoid:** "It pays well" or "there are many jobs" as your only reason.

## Motivation

### "Why do you want to work here?"

**What they want to know:** Did you research the company? Will you stay?

**How to answer:** three precise reasons that link the company to you:

- something about the **product or mission** that interests you;
- the **tech or way of working** (stack, agile, code quality, remote culture);
- **growth**: what you will learn and bring.

**Example answer:**

> Three things. First, your product: I've used your scheduling app, and I like that it solves a real daily problem for small clinics. Second, your engineering culture: I read your blog post about moving to trunk-based development, and I want to work in a team that invests in testing and fast feedback. And third, the role itself — working with Angular and Java on features used by thousands of people matches my experience and where I want to grow.

**Avoid:** generic answers that work for any company ("you are a leader in your field"), talking only about salary or location.

### "What do you know about our company?"

**What they want to know:** Your preparation and real interest.

**How to answer:** summarize what they do, who their customers are, one recent piece of news, and connect it to the role.

**Example answer:**

> You build an e-commerce platform for mid-size retailers, mainly in France and North Africa. I saw you recently launched a mobile app and are expanding to Morocco, which I guess explains the hiring of developers for the front-end team. That growth phase is what attracts me: there's a lot to build.

**Avoid:** reciting the "About us" page; guessing when you don't know — say what you know and ask a question.

### "Why are you leaving your current job?" / "Why did you leave?"

**What they want to know:** Is there a problem with you? Will the same reason make you leave them?

**How to answer:** stay positive and future-focused — talk about what you are looking *for*, not what you are running away *from*.

**Example answer:**

> I've learned a lot in my current company, especially about building reliable APIs. But the product is now in maintenance mode, and I want to work on new features and a more modern stack. This role gives me that opportunity.

**Avoid:** criticizing your manager, colleagues or company; complaining about salary first.

### "Why should we hire you?"

**What they want to know:** Can you summarize your value for *their* needs?

**How to answer:** connect 2–3 requirements of the job to proof from your experience, then add one personal quality.

**Example answer:**

> You need someone who can be productive quickly on Angular and Spring Boot: that's what I've done for three years. You also mentioned performance problems on your dashboard — I worked on exactly that, reducing load time from 6 to 2 seconds with lazy loading and caching. And beyond the technical side, I communicate well with product people, which helps deliver the right thing the first time.

**Avoid:** "I'm a hard worker and motivated" without proof; comparing yourself negatively to other candidates.

## Strengths & weaknesses

### "What are your strengths?"

**What they want to know:** Self-awareness and qualities that matter for the job.

**How to answer:** choose 2–3 strengths relevant to the role and prove each one with a short example.

**Example answer:**

> My main strength is problem solving under uncertainty. For example, when our payment service started failing randomly in production, I organized the investigation, added logging, found a race condition and fixed it within a day. I'm also good at explaining technical topics simply — I regularly run short knowledge-sharing sessions for the team.

**Avoid:** a list of adjectives with no examples; strengths unrelated to the job.

### "What is your greatest weakness?"

**What they want to know:** Honesty, self-awareness, and whether you work on improving.

**How to answer:** a **real but not critical** weakness + what you do concretely to improve + the progress you made.

**Example answer:**

> I used to have trouble saying no, so I took on too many tasks and some deadlines slipped. Now I make my workload visible on the board and discuss priorities with my lead before accepting new work. Since I started doing that, I deliver more predictably and the team knows what I'm working on.

**Avoid:** fake weaknesses ("I'm a perfectionist", "I work too much"); weaknesses that are essential for the job (e.g. "I don't like learning new technologies").

### "How would your colleagues describe you?"

**What they want to know:** How you behave in a team, seen from outside.

**How to answer:** three words with a short example for one or two of them — ideally things a colleague or manager actually said.

**Example answer:**

> Probably reliable, curious and calm. Reliable because when I commit to a deadline I meet it or warn early. And calm — during production incidents my colleagues often say I help the team stay focused.

**Avoid:** qualities that contradict what you said earlier in the interview.

## Behavioral questions (STAR)

### "Tell me about a challenge you faced at work."

**What they want to know:** How you analyze a problem, act and learn — under real conditions.

**How to answer:** use **STAR**. Choose a challenge with a clear result, and spend most of the time on *your* actions.

**Example answer:**

> **Situation:** two weeks before a client demo, our main API became very slow because the database had grown ten times. **Task:** I was responsible for the reporting endpoints, the slowest ones. **Action:** I profiled the queries, found missing indexes and an N+1 problem in the ORM, rewrote the two worst queries and added a cache for the reports. I also set up a dashboard to track response times. **Result:** response time dropped from 8 seconds to under 1 second, the demo went well, and we kept the dashboard to catch regressions.

**Avoid:** challenges with no clear outcome; telling what "the team" did without your own role.

### "Tell me about a mistake you made."

**What they want to know:** Honesty, responsibility and learning. Everyone makes mistakes.

**How to answer:** STAR with a focus on how you **took responsibility**, **fixed** it and what you **changed** afterwards.

**Example answer:**

> Last year I deployed a database migration that locked a big table during working hours, and the app was slow for 20 minutes. I immediately told my lead, rolled back, and re-ran the migration at night in smaller batches. Then I wrote a short checklist for migrations — testing on a copy of production data and running heavy changes off-peak — that the team now uses.

**Avoid:** a mistake that wasn't really yours, or one with no lesson learned; blaming others.

### "Tell me about a conflict with a colleague."

**What they want to know:** Can you disagree professionally and find solutions?

**How to answer:** describe a disagreement about **work** (not personalities), how you listened, used facts, and reached an agreement.

**Example answer:**

> A colleague and I disagreed on whether to rewrite a module or refactor it step by step. The discussion became tense in code review, so I suggested a short call instead. I listened to his reasons — he was worried about the accumulated technical debt — and I explained my concern about the deadline. We agreed to refactor the most critical part now and plan the rest in the next sprint. The release went out on time and we worked well together after that.

**Avoid:** saying you never had conflicts; making the other person look bad.

### "Tell me about a time you worked under pressure or with a tight deadline."

**What they want to know:** Organization and calm when things get hard.

**How to answer:** STAR — show how you **prioritized**, **communicated** and delivered.

**Example answer:**

> A client asked for a new export feature three days before their end-of-year closing. I split the work into must-have and nice-to-have, agreed on the scope with the product owner the same morning, and gave a daily status update. We shipped the core export on time and the extra formats a week later. The client was happy because they knew exactly what to expect.

**Avoid:** suggesting you work best only at the last minute, or that you work nights as a normal solution.

### "What achievement are you most proud of?"

**What they want to know:** What you value, and the level of impact you can have.

**How to answer:** STAR with a measurable result and why it matters to *you*.

**Example answer:**

> I'm proud of the automated test suite I set up for our checkout. There were no tests and every release caused bugs. I wrote the first tests, added them to the CI pipeline and showed the team how to write them. After six months we had 70% coverage on critical paths and production bugs on checkout dropped by more than half. I'm proud because it changed how the whole team works.

**Avoid:** an achievement without your personal contribution; something too old or unrelated.

## Teamwork & work style

### "Do you prefer working alone or in a team?"

**What they want to know:** Whether you fit their way of working.

**How to answer:** show that you can do both, with an example, and lean toward what the job needs.

**Example answer:**

> I like both. I need focused time alone to write good code, but I enjoy the team side — code reviews, pair programming on hard problems, and sharing knowledge. In my last project I worked alone on the search feature but pair-programmed with a colleague for the payment integration because the risk was higher.

**Avoid:** extreme answers ("only alone") unless the job really is solo.

### "How do you handle stress?"

**What they want to know:** Will you stay effective when things go wrong?

**How to answer:** concrete methods + a short example.

**Example answer:**

> I break the problem into small steps and focus on the next one. During incidents I write down what we know and what we've tried, which keeps me calm and helps the team. Outside work, sport helps me keep a good balance.

**Avoid:** "I never get stressed" — it's not credible.

### "How do you keep your skills up to date?"

**What they want to know:** Curiosity and ability to learn — tech changes fast.

**How to answer:** your concrete habits + one recent thing you learned and applied.

**Example answer:**

> I follow a few newsletters and the official blogs of the tools I use, and I build small side projects to try new things. Recently I learned Angular signals on a personal project, and then used them at work to simplify the state of a complex form.

**Avoid:** vague answers ("I read stuff online") with no example.

### "What would you do if you disagreed with your manager's decision?"

**What they want to know:** Respect for hierarchy combined with honesty.

**How to answer:** share your opinion privately with arguments, listen, then commit to the final decision.

**Example answer:**

> I would ask for a short discussion and explain my concerns with facts — for example the risk or cost I see. I'd also try to understand the reasons behind the decision, because my manager may have information I don't. If the decision stays the same, I commit to it fully and help make it work.

**Avoid:** "I'd just do what I'm told" or "I'd do it my way anyway."

## Career goals

### "Where do you see yourself in five years?"

**What they want to know:** Ambition, and whether your goals fit what they can offer.

**How to answer:** a realistic direction (skills, responsibilities) that matches the company's path — not a precise job title.

**Example answer:**

> I see myself as a senior developer with strong expertise in architecture, someone the team relies on for technical decisions, and maybe mentoring junior developers. I'd like to grow inside a company like yours, where the product and the team are growing too.

**Avoid:** "In your job" (to the interviewer), "I don't know", or plans clearly outside the company (starting your own business next year).

### "What are you looking for in your next job?"

**What they want to know:** Whether the job matches your expectations — a bad match means you leave quickly.

**How to answer:** 2–3 criteria that this job actually offers.

**Example answer:**

> A product I can be proud of, a team that cares about code quality and learning, and the chance to take more ownership of features from design to production. From what I've seen in this process, that's what you offer.

**Avoid:** criteria that clearly don't match the job you're interviewing for.

## Salary & practical questions

### "What are your salary expectations?"

**What they want to know:** Whether you fit their budget.

**How to answer:**

- Research the market first (job sites, salary surveys, people in the field) for your level, city and stack.
- Give a **range**, with the bottom being an amount you'd really accept.
- Mention that the full package matters (bonus, remote, training, benefits).

**Example answer:**

> Based on my experience and the market for this kind of role in this city, I'm looking for a range between X and Y per month. That said, I'm also interested in the whole package — training budget and remote days are important to me — so I'm open to discussing it.

**Avoid:** giving a number without research; a range so wide it means nothing; lying about your current salary.

### "When can you start?" / "What is your notice period?"

**What they want to know:** Planning.

**How to answer:** be precise and honest; show respect for your current employer.

**Example answer:**

> My notice period is one month. I'd like to finish my current tasks properly, so I could start on the 1st of next month — and if it helps, I can try to discuss a slightly earlier date with my employer.

**Avoid:** saying you can leave tomorrow without notice — it suggests you could do the same to them.

### "Are you interviewing with other companies?"

**What they want to know:** Your situation and their timing.

**How to answer:** be honest without details, and confirm this job is a priority if it is.

**Example answer:**

> Yes, I'm in a few other processes, which is normal at this stage. But this position is my first choice because of the product and the team, so I'd be happy to move forward quickly.

**Avoid:** naming companies or salaries from other offers to pressure them (unless you are negotiating a real offer).

## Questions to ask the recruiter

**Key ideas**

- Always have **2–3 questions** ready. "No questions" looks like a lack of interest.
- Ask about the **job, team and growth** — not only about holidays and salary in a first interview.
- Good questions:
  - "What does a typical day or week look like in this role?"
  - "What would success look like after the first three or six months?"
  - "How is the team organized, and how do you do code reviews and deployments?"
  - "What are the main technical challenges for the team this year?"
  - "How do you support learning — training, conferences, mentoring?"
  - "What are the next steps in the process?"
- End with a short summary of your interest: "Thank you — after this conversation I'm even more motivated by the role."

**Traps**

- Asking questions whose answers are on the website.
- Asking about salary, holidays or remote work before they show interest — keep it for later steps unless they raise it.
- Forgetting to send a short thank-you email the same day.
